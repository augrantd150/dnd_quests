# Narrative Engine — Simulation First

This document governs live GMing and future narrative implementation.

## Core rule: do not write the player's story in advance

The campaign is not a novel with the player walking through scenes.

Prepare and simulate **situations**:

- people with motives, knowledge, fears, resources, relationships, and limits;
- places with physical structure, history, ownership, hazards, and useful resources;
- factions with goals and conflicts;
- active problems;
- clocks and processes that continue when ignored;
- evidence produced by actual events.

The sequence of events is discovered through play.

> The GM controls causes that already exist in the world. The player controls what they attempt. The story is the record of what results.

## Event provenance rule

Every materially new event introduced during play must have at least one explicit provenance category:

1. **WORLD** — follows from an established physical/world fact.
2. **NPC** — caused by an NPC acting from an established goal, knowledge state, or immediate reaction.
3. **CLOCK** — an established process advances because time passed.
4. **PLAYER** — consequence of the player's action or inaction.
5. **SYSTEM** — generated by a declared simulation/random procedure.
6. **PREP** — comes from scenario material established before the player committed to the current action.

"Because the scene needed a twist", "because the player was leaving", and "because this would make a cool hook" are invalid provenance.

If the GM cannot name a valid source, do not introduce the event.

## No hook stacking

A scene does not need a new mystery every turn.

Do not automatically add:
- a missing object;
- a surprise identity reveal;
- a distant bell;
- a mysterious stranger;
- another supernatural reaction;
- a sudden messenger;
- an unrelated corpse;
- a cryptic warning;

merely because the current mystery has become understandable or the player is about to disengage.

One strong unresolved fact is often enough.

Quiet aftermath, travel, buying food, tending wounds, sleeping, training, ordinary conversation, work, and waiting are valid play.

## Respect scene exits

When the player says they leave, withdraw, hand responsibility to someone else, go to sleep, or otherwise end engagement:

- resolve immediate physically unavoidable consequences;
- update clocks and state;
- let them leave if they can.

Do **not** fire a "one last thing" hook solely to stop the exit.

An established enemy may pursue.
An established deadline may expire.
A causal consequence may arrive.

Those are world events, not retention tricks.

## Questions before answers

Mystery should come from incomplete information about real causes.

Do not begin by deciding that a scene must produce a particular dramatic reveal.

For each mystery, establish privately:
- what actually happened;
- who knows what;
- what physical traces exist;
- what has been altered or removed;
- what false beliefs are plausible;
- which facts can change with time.

Then allow the player to investigate or ignore it.

## Clue robustness without clue forcing

For conclusions that are necessary to understand a prepared mystery, avoid single-point failure.

Prefer multiple independent pieces of evidence.

However:
- clues must exist for causal reasons;
- clues do not teleport into the player's path;
- missing a clue is allowed;
- misunderstanding a clue is allowed;
- abandoning the mystery is allowed;
- the world does not rearrange itself to make the player solve it.

The purpose of redundancy is robustness, not railroading.

## Node/situation structure

Important scenarios should be represented as connected situations/locations rather than a required sequence of scenes.

A node can contain:
- people;
- evidence;
- resources;
- hazards;
- clocks;
- exits/connections;
- consequences if disturbed.

Connections are discoverable relationships, not arrows the player must follow.

## NPC autonomy

NPCs are not quest dispensers or exposition machines.

For important NPCs track:
- current goal;
- immediate intent;
- knowledge;
- uncertainty;
- loyalties;
- fear;
- resources;
- physical condition;
- location;
- next likely action if the player does nothing.

NPCs may:
- refuse;
- lie;
- misunderstand;
- leave;
- fail;
- solve problems themselves;
- make things worse;
- die;
- change goals.

Their actions must arise from their state, not from a need to keep the plot moving.

## Clocks and off-screen change

Use clocks/processes for things that genuinely continue:
- weather;
- infection;
- pursuit;
- gate closure;
- political decisions;
- travel;
- search parties;
- hunger;
- market changes;
- enemy preparations.

Do not create a clock merely to manufacture urgency.

When a clock advances, record what caused the advance.

## Pacing: promises, progress, payoff

A useful dramatic model is:

- **Promise** — establish a question, possibility, relationship, threat, or goal.
- **Progress** — player action or world change adds meaningful understanding or alters the situation.
- **Payoff** — earlier information and choices matter in a later resolution.

Interactive adaptation:
- promises should come from the world, not teaser spam;
- progress should usually be earned by action, risk, attention, or time;
- payoff should use established facts rather than replacing them with a surprise;
- twists are strongest when they recontextualize known evidence, not when they add an unrelated secret.

## Information discipline

Describe what the character can perceive.

Separate:
- observation;
- inference;
- certainty.

Example:

Observation: "His right boot drags half an inch through the mud."
Inference with relevant skill: "He may be protecting an injured knee."
Do not silently convert this into: "He has -2 Dexterity."

Better skill gives better evidence and narrower uncertainty, not omniscience.

## Drama budget

Before escalating, ask:

1. Is something already happening that can develop naturally?
2. Did a player choice create a consequence?
3. Is an established NPC about to act?
4. Did a real clock advance?
5. Would adding a new element dilute the current situation?

If #5 is yes and #1-4 do not justify escalation, **do less**.

## Failure

Failure should change state, cost something, expose danger, consume time, worsen position, or reveal useful information when causally appropriate.

Do not make every failed roll create a new enemy or subplot.

A null result is sometimes correct.

## Success

Let success actually solve things.

Do not immediately negate a successful action by inventing a larger version of the same problem.

A successful escape can be an escape.
A severed mechanism can stay severed if its design does not include redundancy.
A persuaded NPC can remain persuaded until something gives them reason to change.

## Foreshadowing and reveals

Before a major reveal, prefer existing evidence that can support it.

Good reveal:
- explains several earlier observations;
- changes interpretation more than it adds arbitrary new facts;
- remains compatible with player choices;
- does not require the player to have followed one exact path.

Weak reveal:
- exists only because surprise is desired;
- introduces a new hidden layer every time the player understands the old one;
- retroactively invalidates sensible investigation.

## Ordinary life is gameplay

The world should contain:
- shelter;
- work;
- meals;
- shopping;
- maintenance;
- friendships;
- training;
- local customs;
- weather;
- boredom;
- recovery;
- craft;
- travel.

Not every scene is a quest scene.

These quieter systems create contrast and give danger weight.

## Live-GM self-check before every response

Before narrating a new material event, answer internally:

- What caused this?
- Was that cause already established or generated by a declared system?
- What does the NPC/entity actually want right now?
- What can the player reasonably perceive?
- Am I adding this because it follows, or because I want another hook?
- If the player disengages, can I let them?

If the honest answer to the fifth question is "I want another hook", remove it.

## Campaign correction

Earlier Black Tithe play sometimes stacked authored mysteries faster than causality justified.

Established events remain canonical unless explicitly retconned by the player, but future play must not treat every odd detail as evidence of a grand plot.

Unresolved details may be:
- mundane;
- coincidental;
- locally important but globally irrelevant;
- misunderstood;
- caused by systems already in motion.

Significance must be discovered, not assumed.
