# Design Pillars

## 1. Lethal but fair

The world is not an amusement park built around the player's current power.

A novice can meet a veteran, a pack of armed men, a starving beast, a plague house, or an occult event that is beyond them. The game must allow scouting, negotiation, avoidance, retreat, preparation, deception, and refusal.

The fairness contract is:

> Dangerous things should usually leave readable evidence before they kill you.

Examples:
- tracks reveal weight and number;
- armor and posture reveal training;
- corpses reveal the kind of violence nearby;
- silence, smell, animal behavior, architecture, weather, wounds, rumors, and missing people can all be information.

The game must not guarantee that every clue is noticed, understood, or still available.

## 2. High skill floor is optional; high skill ceiling is mandatory

A new player should be able to act.

An expert player should still discover better timing, positioning, combinations, routes, preparations, resource trades, and opponent reads hundreds of encounters later.

Skill expression should come from interacting systems, not memorizing a single optimal combo.

## 3. Weird utility beats flat damage inflation

The most exciting abilities should alter **relationships** in the encounter:
- motion;
- route;
- line of sight;
- timing;
- access;
- pressure;
- information;
- terrain;
- stored energy;
- target priority;
- escape options.

Raw damage is useful, but the signature fantasy is turning an apparently bad situation by understanding it better.

## 4. Causal magic

Magic belongs to the same world as steel, rain, blood, stone, fire, momentum, disease, and fear.

For an important magical event the simulation should be able to answer:
- what happened;
- what caused it;
- what was required;
- what was transferred or transformed;
- what resisted it;
- what its limits were;
- what failure looked like;
- what physical state exists afterward.

## 5. Visible progression

The player must be able to see improvement.

Progress should be attributable:
- "Footwork increased because you repeatedly maintained distance under genuine pressure."
- "Transfer Control improved after three difficult supervised exercises."
- "Anatomy understanding improved by treating real wounds and studying a corpse."

No invisible global scaling.

## 6. Persistent consequences

Wounds, broken doors, missing items, deaths, debts, burned buildings, rumors, faction memory, discovered passages, and learned information persist.

A quest is a world situation, not a disposable script.

## 7. Solo-first design

The game must remain deep with one player character.

NPC allies may exist, but core encounters cannot assume a traditional four-person party will cover every weakness.

This makes preparation, escape, information, tools, hirelings, terrain, and non-combat solutions especially important.

## 8. Dark medieval, not meaningless misery

The setting is harsh: hunger, feudal power, disease, superstition, violence, dangerous travel, winter, bad medicine, and religious or occult institutions matter.

But darkness needs contrast. Warmth, humor, friendship, craft, strange beauty, festivals, kindness, and absurd human behavior make losses matter.

## 9. Systemic absurdity is allowed

Serious rules can produce bizarre outcomes.

A shrine used as a kinetic anchor, a funeral bell that becomes an investigative instrument, or a retreat route turned into an ambush can be funny or strange while the simulation remains rigorous.

## 10. No plot armor

The GM or simulation must not alter a resolved result because death would be inconvenient.

If the character dies, the campaign state records it.

## 11. Situation over plot

The game prepares people, places, motives, evidence, resources, hazards, and processes—not a required sequence of dramatic beats.

The player is allowed to:
- walk away;
- solve something early;
- misunderstand something;
- ignore a mystery permanently;
- choose an ordinary life activity instead;
- cause a situation to develop in an unexpected direction.

The simulation must not create replacement hooks merely to restore a planned story.

## 12. Significance is earned

Do not make every unusual object, line of dialogue, NPC, or environmental detail part of one central conspiracy.

Some things are local.
Some are mundane.
Some are coincidence.
Some are meaningful only because the player makes them meaningful.

Patterns should arise from shared causes, not authorial compulsion.
